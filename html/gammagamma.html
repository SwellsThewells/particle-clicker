You saw light scattering off light!

Photons that collide

In classical physics, two beams of light pass straight through each other. In the 1930s, physicists working on quantum electrodynamics, the quantum theory of light and electric charge, predicted that photons can bounce off each other: for a brief moment a photon can turn into a pair of charged particles, and the other photon can scatter off them.

The effect is tiny. ATLAS saw the first signs of it in 2016, and in 2019 it observed the process with a significance of 8.2 standard deviations: 59 candidate events, where only 12 were expected from other processes.

Collisions that miss

The photons come from lead ions. Each lead nucleus has 82 protons, so it is surrounded by an enormous electric field that behaves like a cloud of photons. When two lead ions fly past each other without touching, in what physicists call an ultra-peripheral collision, their photons can collide.

What is left is an almost empty detector, apart from two photons flying off in opposite directions.
