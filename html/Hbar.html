You made antihydrogen!

An atom of antimatter

Antihydrogen is the antimatter twin of hydrogen: a positron, the antiparticle of the electron, bound to an antiproton.

The first nine atoms were made at CERN in 1995. They were moving at nearly the speed of light and vanished almost immediately. In 2002 the ATHENA and ATRAP experiments at CERN's Antiproton Decelerator produced thousands of slow antihydrogen atoms.

Catching antimatter

Antimatter annihilates as soon as it touches ordinary matter, so antihydrogen has to be held in a vacuum by magnetic fields. The ALPHA experiment first trapped antihydrogen atoms in 2010, and has since measured the light they emit with great precision. So far it looks exactly like hydrogen.

In 2023 the ALPHA-g experiment showed that antihydrogen falls down under gravity, just like ordinary matter. Physicists keep looking for a difference, because it might explain why our universe is made almost entirely of matter.
