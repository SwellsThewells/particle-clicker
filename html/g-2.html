You measured the magnetism of the muon!

A tiny spinning magnet

The muon, a heavier cousin of the electron, behaves like a tiny spinning magnet. How strong a magnet it is for its spin is given by a number called g. Dirac's equation says that g is exactly 2.

But empty space is not really empty: virtual particles keep popping in and out of existence around the muon and change g very slightly. Physicists measure the difference, g − 2, because every known particle, and perhaps unknown ones too, leaves its mark on it.

From CERN to Fermilab

CERN measured the muon's g − 2 in three experiments between 1959 and 1979. The last one kept muons circling in a storage ring at a "magic" speed, where the ring's electric fields do not disturb their spin. Later experiments at Brookhaven and Fermilab used the same trick.

For about twenty years, the measurements seemed a little higher than the prediction of the Standard Model, a possible hint of new particles. In 2025 Fermilab published its final, most precise result. New calculations on supercomputers, using lattice QCD, now bring the prediction close to it, so the famous anomaly seems to be fading.
