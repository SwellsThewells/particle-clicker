Shape-shifting ghosts

Neutrinos come in three flavours: electron, muon and tau neutrinos. They hardly ever interact with matter; trillions of them pass through your body every second without leaving a trace.

In 1998 the Super-Kamiokande detector in Japan, a huge tank of ultra-pure water a kilometre underground, counted muon neutrinos made in the atmosphere. Fewer arrived from below, after crossing the whole Earth, than from above. On their long journey, some had turned into a different flavour.

Why it matters

A neutrino can only change its flavour if neutrinos have mass. In the original Standard Model they were massless, so neutrino oscillations were the first clear sign of physics beyond it.

In 2001 the Sudbury Neutrino Observatory in Canada showed that neutrinos from the Sun change flavour as well, solving the long-standing solar neutrino problem. Takaaki Kajita and Arthur B. McDonald shared the 2015 Nobel Prize in Physics for these discoveries.
